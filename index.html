Research

RelayMoE: training MoE models without the all-to-all memory spike

Memory-Efficient Expert Routing for Distributed MoE Training, under review, 2026 (arXiv)

Where the memory goes

In a Mixture-of-Experts model, each token is sent to its top-k experts, and those experts usually live on different GPUs. The standard way to move tokens is an all-to-all exchange: every GPU packs the tokens bound for every other GPU, sends them, and receives everything it needs at once.

That means each GPU materializes the full top-k-expanded token buffer in one go. As models use more experts, larger k and longer sequences, this buffer, not attention, becomes the peak of memory use. It caps how long a sequence you can train and how large a batch fits on a fixed set of GPUs.

Passing the work around a ring

RelayMoE replaces the one-shot exchange with a ring. GPUs are arranged in a ring, and at each step every GPU computes on what it currently holds, then passes it to its neighbour. No GPU ever needs the full dispatch buffer, only one slice at a time.

  • Adaptive routing. Either the tokens or the expert weights can travel. RelayMoE picks whichever moves fewer bytes for the layer at hand.
  • Overlap. Sending the next slice runs while the current slice is being computed, so the ring hides most of its communication.
  • Cheaper backward pass. The same ring structure supports memory-efficient recomputation during backpropagation.

On 8B to 57B MoE models, this gives up to 2.02× throughput and 2.85× longer trainable sequences than Megatron-LM, DeepEP, Tutel and FasterMoE. This work is in collaboration with the Barcelona Supercomputing Center.

HalfGNN: making half precision work for graph neural networks

Optimization of GNN Training Through Half-precision, HPDC 2025 (paper, arXiv)

Why half precision broke GNN training

Half precision (FP16) should make training faster and use less memory. For GNNs, existing systems got the opposite: their sparse kernels (SpMM and SDDMM) ran no faster, or slower, than in FP32, and models such as GCN and GIN trained to abnormally low accuracy.

Two causes stood out. GNN aggregation sums the features of many neighbours, and for high-degree nodes that running sum overflows FP16's range before it is ever normalized. And naive mixed-precision setups kept converting tensors between FP16 and FP32 and falling back to FP32 kernels, which threw away the speed benefit.

Discretized reduction and half-precision vector types

  • Discretized reduction. Instead of summing all of a node's neighbours and scaling at the end, the kernels reduce a fixed-size batch of products, scale it, and repeat. Values stay in range, so overflow can't happen, and the fixed batches also balance work across threads.
  • Vector types. New half2, half4 and half8 operations load, store and reduce several FP16 values at once, so the kernels use the GPU's memory bandwidth properly.
  • FP16 end to end. Every kernel in the backend runs in half precision, with non-atomic writes through a staging buffer, so there is no constant conversion back to FP32.

Together, these give an average 2.3× faster training than float-based DGL for GCN, GIN and GAT, with 2.67× less memory and the same accuracy.
